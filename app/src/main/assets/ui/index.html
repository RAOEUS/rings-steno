Dictionaries

Each system keeps its own stack. The top dictionary wins, as in Plover.

Add files

Or share a dictionary file to Rings from any other app, or open one with Rings. It takes .json, .py, .modal and .rtf files, and .zip files of them. A plugin's .whl file is added the same way.

Plugins

Plover's own plugins, loaded as Plover loads them. To add one, get its .whl file (on PyPI, under Download files) and share or open it with Rings, or use Add files. A plugin runs as code inside Rings, so add only ones you trust. Only pure Python plugins can run on a phone.

A steno board for your thumbs

Each thumb has one stick. It opens wherever that thumb lands on its half, so there is nothing to aim at. The directions are the board itself: push toward where the key sits. Keys add up until the stroke is sent, a moment after both thumbs are off the glass. That is the only clock: how long a thumb rests never changes what its push means.

short push
Your hand's block of six, as on the board: T P H over K W R on the left, -F -P -L over -R -B -G on the right. Push toward a key and lift. Push again for another key from the block.
long push
Keep going past the block and you reach the keys beyond it, where the board has them. The single keys sit out beyond the gaps between the block's keys, so sliding out along a gap touches nothing on the way: S- out to the left between T and K, * out to the right between H and R, A down between K and W, O down between W and R. On the right it is -S and -Z out to the sides, E and U down through the gaps, -T and -D up through them. Straight down through the middle key is both vowels; straight up is the number bar on the left, -T with -D on the right. A strong double tick tells you the push went long. A long push gives the far key in place of any near one, never both.
tap
A quick tap is the stick's click: S- on the left, * on the right. A right tap on its own undoes the last stroke.
one thumb
Thumbs in Settings, or the two-ring button beside the keyboard's tape, gives you a single stick for writing with one hand. It starts every stroke on the left keys; a quick tap swaps it to the right keys. A tap on the right keys is the star and takes you back to the left, so two taps make the star wherever you are. Two ticks say right, one says left, and the pad changes colour. With one thumb S is a long push left on the left keys, and the star is there too, out to the right.
dead zones
Nothing counts until your thumb has clearly left the spot where it landed. A thumb that rests, or slides a little and lifts, does nothing. The gaps between the block's keys are dead as well: a short push that ends in one is nothing, never a guess. Rolling onto a neighbour takes a clear move, so a wobble adds no key. A longer tick tells you each stroke has gone and the next can start.
near an edge
The stick is cut off flat where it meets the edge, so all of it stays on the screen. Every key keeps its direction and its width; only pushes toward the edge are shorter, by as much as the room allows. The double tick still marks where long begins.

More than one key from the block

one by one
Push, lift, push again. Each push adds its key, so the rolls below are shortcuts you can leave for later.
neighbours
Roll from one key onto the next and you get both, in either direction. Keep rolling and every key you cross is added. The same goes for the keys beyond the block: -T rolled across to -D, A down round to O. A roll stays in the ring it started in, however far it strays.
go back over
Roll back onto a key you have already crossed and it is taken out again: T, P, H, then back onto P, is TH. That is the whole rule. Settings has two others if you prefer them: plover_controller's, where rolling against steno order keeps only the two ends, and one where every key you cross simply stays in.
or two trips
Without lifting, come back to the centre and push out again, short or long.

Arpeggiate, for learning

Switch it on in Settings and the clock stops. Each push adds its keys and they stay down while you find the next one. A short push goes to the block, a long push to the keys beyond it, and pushing a key that is already down takes it out. Back undoes your last push and Clear empties the stroke, so a slip never has to be sent. Press Send when the stroke is right, the way the space bar does in Plover's arpeggiate. Taps are the sticks' clicks as usual: S- on the left, * on the right, so a right tap and Send undoes the last stroke.

The block's six directions are laid out as on plover_controller. KAT is a short push down-left for K, a long push down-left for A, and on the right a long push up-left for -T. The tape shows the stroke you are building in red.

What does the writing

The engine is Plover itself, by the Open Steno Project, running inside the app with its own plugins: translation, undo, spelling rules, commands, Python and modal dictionaries all behave as they do on a computer because they are the same code. The six-way layout of the block, and the Backward skips roll rule, are the default mapping of plover_controller by Tadeo Kondrak and contributors.
